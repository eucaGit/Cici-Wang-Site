Buying a Remodeled Home in Mountain View or Los Altos: A Permit-Record Checklist

A refreshed kitchen or added room deserves a closer look at its renovation history. Organize seller documents, city permit records, and unanswered questions before making your next decision.

A bright new kitchen, an added room, or a converted garage can make it easy to picture life in a home. After the tour, take another useful step: organize the documents behind the changes you noticed.

This checklist is for households comparing remodeled homes in Mountain View and Los Altos, and for sellers preparing a clear record of past improvements.

1. Start with the changes that matter to you

List the work you want to understand: a different kitchen layout, an additional bathroom, an extension, or a change in how the garage is used. Create three columns beside each item: seller documents, city records, and questions still open. Keep observations separate from conclusions.

2. Match documents to the work

Ask your agent to help request available permits, plans, inspection records, and relevant disclosures. Compare the address, scope of work, dates, and permit numbers. Invoices and finished-room photographs can add context, but they should not be used alone to determine the approval or inspection history of an entire project.

3. Begin with official city resources

Mountain View’s Research Permit History page provides research guidance and notes that not all building permit records are available online. If an online search does not turn up a document, ask the city how to continue the search rather than treating a missing result as proof that no permit exists.

The City of Los Altos maintains a Monthly Issued Permits archive. It offers one starting point for locating issued permits. Follow up with city staff about a particular property’s scope of work, inspections, and completion records; the monthly list is a research lead rather than a complete property review.

4. Turn discrepancies into specific questions

Suppose a document describes a kitchen remodel, but the home also has an added room. Which records relate to that room? What does a status field mean? Are additional inspections or documents needed? Direct each question to someone who can confirm the answer and record the response, date, and source. For questions about construction quality, arrange an appropriate professional assessment. Permit research does not replace an on-site inspection.

5. Connect the research to your transaction plan

Discuss the documents needed, who will request them, and which questions remain unresolved with your agent. Consider the answers alongside your move and renovation plans. Treat a future addition, change of use, or permit approval as an unresolved possibility until it has been properly evaluated.

For sellers, organizing this information early can also make buyer questions easier to address: describe the improvements, identify available documents, and make unresolved items clear.
